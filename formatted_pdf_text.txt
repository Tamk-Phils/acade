1.1 Background
Phishing and social engineering remain among the most persistent and financially
damaging classes of cyberattack, exploiting human trust and judgement rather than
purely technical vulnerabilities. Attackers routinely impersonate trusted organisations
15

through email, text messages (smishing), voice calls (vishing) and, increasingly,
synthetic media such as deepfake audio and video, in order to induce victims to disclose
credentials, transfer funds or install malicious software. Industry reporting consistently
identifies phishing as the most frequently used initial attack vector in data breaches, and
business email compromise alone has been linked to tens of billions of dollars in
reported losses over the past decade. The last few years have seen a marked shift in the
sophistication of these attacks, driven by the same advances in artificial intelligence
(AI) that are used defensively. Generative language models can now produce
grammatically flawless, contextually convincing phishing emails at scale, while voicecloning and video-synthesis tools lower the barrier for convincing impersonation attacks
against employees, executives and customers. At the same time, AI and machine
learning (ML) have proven effective on the defensive side, enabling systems that
classify malicious URLs, detect anomalous email content, and flag suspicious
behavioural patterns with a level of speed and consistency that manual or purely rulebased approaches cannot match. Despite this progress, most existing AI-based defences
address a single, narrow slice of the problem — for example, URL reputation scoring or
spam-style email filtering — implemented as isolated tools rather than as part of a
coherent system architecture. Organisations, particularly small and medium-sized ones
in resource-constrained environments such as Cameroon, are left to assemble ad-hoc
combinations of point solutions, with little unified visibility across email, web, and
voice/social channels, and with limited capability to detect the broader socialengineering tactics (pretexting, baiting, authority impersonation) that increasingly
accompany AI-generated phishing content. This proposal is concerned with the design
of a unified, AI-powered system architecture that brings detection, analysis and user
awareness into a single platform.
1.2 Description of the Research Problem
Existing phishing-detection tools are largely single-purpose: a spam filter analyses
email headers and text, a URL scanner checks domain reputation, and securityawareness training is delivered separately as a human-resources exercise. These
components rarely share data or a common decision layer, which creates blind spots —
an attack that combines a legitimate-looking email with a spoofed voice call, for
instance, is unlikely to be caught by any single existing tool. Furthermore, the rise of
AI-generated phishing content undermines detection approaches that rely heavily on
stylistic or grammatical anomalies, since such anomalies are precisely what generative
16

models are able to eliminate. There is, therefore, a need for a system architecture that (i)
integrates multiple AI/ML detection engines — natural-language content analysis,
URL/domain analysis, and behavioural/contextual analysis — into a single, modular
platform; (ii) is designed to scale and to be extensible to new channels and attack types;
and (iii) closes the loop with users through real-time reporting and awareness feedback
rather than treating detection and human training as separate concerns. This proposal
addresses that gap by proposing, and partially prototyping, such an architecture.
1.3 Research Questions and Objectives
1.3.1 Research Questions
RQ1: What are the architectural and detection limitations of existing AI-based phishing
and social-engineering countermeasures? RQ2: What modular system architecture can
integrate multiple AI/ML detection engines and a user-facing awareness component into
a single, scalable phishing and social-engineering detection platform? RQ3: To what
extent does a prototype implementation of the proposed architecture achieve acceptable
detection accuracy, response latency and scalability under test conditions?
1.3.2 Objectives
General Objective: To design an AI-powered system architecture for a unified phishing
detection and social-engineering awareness platform, and to validate its core detection
components through a working prototype.
Specific Objectives:
To review and critically analyse existing AI-based approaches to phishing and socialengineering detection, and to identify their architectural and functional limitations. To
design a modular, layered system architecture comprising data-ingestion, featureextraction, an ensemble of ML/NLP detection engines, a decision and alerting layer, and
a user-facing dashboard/reporting module. To develop a prototype implementing the
core detection engine(s) of the proposed architecture using publicly available phishing
datasets. To evaluate the prototype's detection performance (accuracy, precision, recall,
F1-score) and system-level performance (latency, throughput) against baseline
approaches.
1.4 Rationale
1.4.1 Justification and Motivation
17

The motivation for this study stems from the widening gap between the sophistication
of AI-enabled social-engineering attacks and the fragmented, single-purpose nature of
most deployed defences, particularly in institutions with limited security budgets. A
well-designed, modular architecture that unifies detection across channels can be
adopted incrementally and extended over time, offering a more sustainable defensive
posture than a collection of disconnected tools.
1.4.2 Significance
The study is significant to (i) organisations seeking a practical reference architecture for
consolidating phishing and social-engineering defences; (ii) the academic community,
by contributing an evaluated architectural design and prototype to the growing body of
work on AI-based cybersecurity systems; and (iii) the candidate, as partial fulfilment of
the requirements for the award of the M.Tech in Information Technology and
Cybersecurity.
1.5 Scope and Limitation
1.5.1 Scope
The study covers the design of a system architecture for AI-powered phishing and
social-engineering detection, spanning email/text content analysis, URL/domain
analysis and a user reporting/awareness module. A prototype will be developed to
validate the feasibility of the core detection engine(s) using publicly available phishing
datasets.
1.5.2 Limitation
The study is limited by the availability of large-scale, labelled datasets covering voiceand video-based social-engineering attacks, which restricts full prototyping of those
channels; the prototype will therefore emphasise text- and URL-based detection, with
voice/video analysis addressed at the architectural (design) level only. Computing
resources available to the candidate may also constrain the scale of model training and
load-testing that can be performed. CHAPTER 2LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Conceptual Overview
Phishing is commonly defined as a form of social engineering in which an attacker
impersonates a trustworthy entity, typically through electronic communication, in order
to induce a victim to disclose sensitive information or perform a harmful action. Social
18

engineering is the broader category of manipulation techniques — including pretexting,
baiting and impersonation — of which phishing (and its variants, smishing and vishing)
is the most common digital expression. Recent scholarship increasingly frames phishing
detection within this broader social-engineering context rather than as a purely technical
email- or URL-classification problem.
2.2 Traditional and List-Based Detection Approaches
Early phishing defences relied on blacklists and heuristic rule sets — comparing URLs
and domains against known-malicious lists, or flagging emails containing certain
keywords. These approaches are simple and low-cost but struggle against previously
unseen (zero-day) phishing sites and cannot adapt automatically as attackers change
tactics, motivating the shift towards machine-learning-based detection.
2.3 Machine Learning and Deep Learning Approaches
A substantial body of recent work applies supervised machine learning to phishing
detection, typically using features extracted from URLs, page content, or email headers
and bodies, with classifiers such as Random Forest, Support Vector Machines, and
gradient-boosted trees reported to achieve strong detection accuracy on benchmark
datasets. Alnemari and Alshammari (2023) demonstrate this approach for phishingdomain classification, achieving high accuracy using ensemble learning on domainlevel features. Similarly, Divakarla and Chandrasekaran (2023) apply machine-learning
algorithms across both email and website features to improve detection robustness.
Deep learning methods, including convolutional and recurrent neural networks, have
subsequently been applied to capture more complex patterns in email text and page
structure, and are generally reported to outperform classical models on larger, more
diverse datasets, at the cost of reduced interpretability and higher computational
demand. Kavya and Sumathi (2024) provide a comprehensive review of this trajectory,
tracing the field from list-based techniques through machine learning to deep-learning
and graph-based methods, and highlighting persistent challenges around dataset
diversity and adversarial robustness — that is, the vulnerability of trained models to
inputs deliberately crafted to evade them.
2.4 AI-Enabled Social Engineering and Synthetic Media
A newer strand of literature examines how generative AI is being used offensively,
producing highly convincing phishing text and, increasingly, synthetic voice and video
19

content used for impersonation. Mirsky and Lee (2021) provide a comprehensive survey
of deepfake creation and detection techniques, cataloguing the generative methods
behind synthetic audio and video and the corresponding detection strategies, which is
directly relevant to the voice/video dimension of social engineering. Complementary
work by Schmitt and Flechais (2024) examines how generative AI lowers the barrier to
crafting convincing social-engineering content, underscoring the need for defences that
are not solely dependent on the stylistic or grammatical cues that earlier detectors relied
upon.
2.5 Datasets and System Architectures
Publicly available datasets such as the UCI Phishing Websites dataset, PhishTank feeds,
and labelled phishing-email corpora (e.g., the Vrbančič phishing-websites dataset, 2020)
remain the most commonly used resources for training and benchmarking detection
models. However, the reviewed literature is dominated by model-level contributions (a
classifier trained and benchmarked on one dataset) rather than complete system
architectures that integrate multiple detection engines, threat-intelligence feeds and
user-facing components into a single deployable platform. Where architectures are
discussed, they are typically presented at a high level, without detailed treatment of
scalability, modularity, or the integration of a human-awareness/reporting loop.
2.6 Research Gap
The reviewed literature confirms that AI/ML techniques for phishing detection are well
established at the model level, and that AI-enabled social engineering (including
synthetic media) is a recognised and growing threat. What is comparatively underaddressed is the system-architecture layer: a modular, extensible design that combines
multiple detection engines across channels with a real-time user-reporting and
awareness component, suited to deployment in resource-constrained institutional
settings. This proposal addresses that gap. CHAPTER 3MATERIALS AND
METHODS (PROPOSED METHODOLOGY)
3.1 Research Design
The study will adopt a Design Science Research (DSR) approach, appropriate for
research whose principal contribution is an artefact — in this case, a system architecture
and an accompanying prototype. The DSR cycle followed will comprise: (i) problem
identification and objective definition (Chapter 1); (ii) design and development of the
20

architecture and prototype; (iii) demonstration of the prototype against representative
datasets; and (iv) evaluation against defined performance metrics, with findings feeding
back into refinement of the design.
3.2 Proposed System Architecture Overview
The proposed architecture will be organised into five logical layers: Data Ingestion
Layer: connectors for email (via IMAP/API), submitted URLs, and user-reported
messages, normalising incoming items into a common internal representation. Feature
Extraction Layer: NLP-based text feature extraction (e.g., tokenisation, embedding
generation) for message content, and lexical/host-based feature extraction (e.g., domain
age, WHOIS data, URL structure) for links. Detection Engine Layer: an ensemble of
ML/NLP models — a text-classification model for message content and a URL/domainclassification model — whose outputs are combined by a decision-fusion component to
produce a single risk score. Decision and Alerting Layer: applies configurable risk
thresholds, triggers alerts, and logs decisions for audit and model-retraining purposes.
Presentation Layer: a dashboard for administrators (aggregate threat visibility, model
performance) and a lightweight reporting/feedback interface for end users, closing the
loop between detection and user awareness. This layered, modular design is intended to
allow individual detection engines to be replaced or extended (for example, adding a
voice/video-analysis engine) without requiring changes to the rest of the platform.
3.3 Data Collection and Datasets
The prototype will be trained and evaluated using publicly available, ethically sourced
datasets, including the UCI Phishing Websites dataset, PhishTank-derived URL feeds,
the Vrbančič (2020) phishing-websites dataset, and publicly available labelled phishingemail corpora. No proprietary or personally identifiable data will be collected from real
users during the prototype phase.
3.4 Tools and Technologies
Programming and modelling: Python, with Scikit-learn for classical ML models and
TensorFlow/PyTorch for deep-learning experiments. NLP processing: standard textpreprocessing and embedding libraries (e.g., spaCy, Hugging Face Transformers) for
message-content analysis. Service architecture: a lightweight API framework (e.g.,
FastAPI/Flask) exposing detection engines as independent services, containerised with
Docker for portability. Dashboard: a web-based front end for visualising detection
21

results and collecting user reports. Version control and documentation: Git for source
control and Markdown/LaTeX-style documentation of the design artefacts.
3.5 Model Development
Model development will proceed in two parallel tracks corresponding to the two
primary detection engines: (i) a text-classification model trained on labelled
phishing/legitimate email and message content, using both classical ML baselines (e.g.,
Random Forest, SVM) and a deep-learning model for comparison; and (ii) a
URL/domain-classification model trained on lexical and host-based features. Model
selection will be guided by comparative performance on a held-out validation split,
informed by the approaches reviewed in Chapter 2.
3.6 Evaluation Metrics
The prototype will be evaluated using standard classification metrics — accuracy,
precision, recall and F1-score — computed on a held-out test set, together with systemlevel metrics of response latency and throughput measured under simulated load. Where
feasible, the combined (ensemble) detection pipeline will be compared against each
individual engine to quantify the benefit of the proposed multi-engine architecture.
3.7 Ethical Considerations
The study will use only publicly available, de-identified datasets for model training and
evaluation. No real phishing campaigns will be conducted against human subjects, and
any demonstration or user-reporting feature will be tested using synthetic or consented
sample data only, in line with the University's research-ethics expectations and the
COLTECH guidance on academic integrity.
